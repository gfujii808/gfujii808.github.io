Design Notebook | Silhouette Studio + Vinyl Plotter
Week 1 · 09 / 22

Goals: To create a personalized design notebook with the Silhouette Studio software and a Cricut-like sticker machine. The assignment minimum is to have at least 1 non-practice double-layer vinyl sticker.

Description: This project was an introduction to the sticker-making process and Maker Lab as a whole. We followed the following process: Design → Cutting → Weeding → Transferring top layer onto bottom layer → Stickering. The design I chose was a cute/whimsical little mushroom with a froggy friend. I started with the mushroom, as I wanted to have something that I could design on Inkscape with simple shapes (rectangle, oval) before sending the components (red base, white dots) to Silhouette Studio. Then, I thought the mushroom would be lonely, so I chose to trace a free SVG of a frog directly in Silhouette Studio. The background is a leaf-paisley inspired pattern, also from Creative Commons, that is meant to invoke a whimsical forest vibe while keeping a more stylized repetition that allows the frog and mushroom to be the main focal point. The window was made in Inkscape, where I again took advantage of the boolean and bitmap tracing tools to crate my final cuts (images 6 and 7). The cut settings and transfer from Inkscape to Silhouette Studio were a bit painful to figure out, but I think the final results are worth it :) Image 8 depicts Dr. JAK's sacrificial notebook, who took the abuse of my trial and error with settings, which ended up being those shown in Image 9.

Time Log: Each sticker took about 20 minutes to complete, from the designing to the actual sticker-ing. The window, from sourcing to cutting, took about an hour, as finding visually beautiful and physically feasible (able to cut a window, not just a giant hole) design and getting it traced and boolean-ed properly was a long process.

Reflection: From this experience, I learned how to use the sticker machine and Silhouette Studio software. In Silhouette Studio, I initially had trouble importing my mushroom design as an SVG, so resorted to a PNG (image 2). From there, the PNG was unable to be separated for cutting (cutting one color on the left of the cutting board, another color on the right of the cutting board), and tracing was not an option, as my Inkscape project used hairline (no tolerances could find the lines). From this, I learned to use the eraser tool and to use thicker lines if I wanted to trace images. I also used the 'trace bitmap' tool in Inkscape with the boolean tool quite a bit to create the appropriate window cut-out for the cover.

LED Acrylic Sign | Epilog Laser Cutter
Week 2 · 10 / 01

Goals: To create an LED sign with the laser cutter and clear acrylic. The light will do its thing (thanks, physics) to illuminate rastered portions and highlight cut edges. The assignment minimum is to have at least 1 rastered (etched) element and 1 vectored (cut) element.

Description: This project was an introduction to the laser cutter. I followed the following process: Measurement → Ideation → Design → Test Cut → Actual Cut. I took a more personalized turn for this project, and I wanted to look more into my connection with Hawaiian culture. Hawaiian quilts are particularly significant, and the pattern of a quilt tells a story. I drew up my own quilt pattern (Image 1): monstera leaves that I’ve always been fascinated by, lavender for my mother’s favorite flower, and naupaka, an Indigenous Hawaiian flower whose story I’m quite fond of. I wanted to play more with boolean tools on Inkscape, so I used simple ovals and rectangles to boolean-out my cut lines that trace around the outer bounds of the design (Images 7&8). The thick inner design is to be rastered. The learning curve for this one wasn't so bad, because I'm familiar with the laser cutter - I've made some coasters before :)

Time Log: Ideation took forever (~1 hr) because I had no ideas at first. Once I had the idea of the quilt, things moved a lot more. The designing took a while (~1 hr) because I wanted a DIY quilt pattern, but once I had a workable PNG, I got to do my favorite thing – boolean! I booleaned the daylights out of the ovals and rectangles that would outline my quilt to create the cut outline. Once I got files onto the Maker Lab computer, I was able to fight with file types and get all my prints done in an hour. I had a settings tester (quilt design keychain version), a functionality tester (ALOHA design), insert redesign tester (the weird little bumpy thing), and the glorious final quilt design with the correct insert dimensions.

Reflection: This was a relatively painless process, the only frictions being dongles, remembering to use PDF file types for transfer, and not using tools that make Corel crash out (crop tool). I had fun drafting designs, booleaning (love a good boolean), and watching the laser run around :)

2D to 3D | Epilog Laser Cutter
Week 4 · 10 / 13

Goals: To design a fully press-fit 3D artifact using the laser cutter + 1/8" plywood. The assignment requires a kerf-bent or box-jointed structure and at least one rastered element.

Description: I followed the following process: Measurement → Ideation → Design → Test Cut → Corrections → Pivot to Backup Plan→ Final Cuts → Assembly. This project started as Awapuhi in a Pot (Images 1-4). Awapuhi, Hawaiian ginger, grows in every auntie’s backyard and is a kitchen-table staple that lasts weeks even when cut. That “unkillable plant” energy felt like the right thing to build. The first pot design (Images 5-7) was a finger-jointed box generated in boxes.py — 65mm square, 3mm plywood, rounded corners, shrunk manually to 57.5mm tall. The top had a radial series of cutouts for the 8 stems to slot into. The 8-panel radial design looked great on paper, but was genuinely not going to work in real life (Image 12). The slots were too large, and the flower stems sat offset from the flower’s center, which made the whole thing look a bit rough. With the pivot, the design is simpler, cleaner, and honestly better: a boxes.py FlexBox pot (sx=70, y=60, h=60, outside) with lei designs (designed by me!!) rastered on all four wall panels to have a more cohesive piece (Image 9). The flower design is also simplified for a fit that actually works and can be switched out with christmas tree and pua kalaunu (milkweed flower) inserts (Images 10, 11).

Time Log: Ideation was pretty quick (1hr), but the designing of flower components to make something physically feasible and visually appealing took some effort in and out of class(3 hrs). My ‘MVP’ plan of the slotting 2-piece plant inserts (1.5 hr) was much less painful than the radial 8-stem idea. Each round of cuts (3) took 30 minutes from starting up the laptop to grabbing the pieces off the cut bed, and everything was cut outside of classtime except for the final two-piece inserts (Image 8).

Reflection: I appreciated the feedback from our classtime pitching before we started the actual project, as it gave me time to come up with a scaffolded plan to "go for broke," react to issues, and have a backup plan. I also learned that overcompensating tolerance is just as annoying as undercompensating, and that physical constraints of assemblies should not be overlooked in the quest for aesthetics! I’m really glad I had the slotting 2-piece insert idea so that simplifying my design went quickly.

Phone Stand | Prusa 3D printer and Slicer
Week 6 · 10 / 27

Goals: To design and 3D print a functional phone stand with space for charging cords using the Prusa MK4(S) and PrusaSlicer.

Description: For my first-ever 3D printing adventure, I wanted to find a simple and very portable design that I could take with me on-the-go. I followed the following process: Ideation → Reference → Modeling → Slicing → Print. Since the Calibration Cat was my choice of calibration object, I chose to follow the cat theme and adapt a Thingiverse phone stand to look like a cat silhouette. The stand body is 172×170×9mm flat-printed with whisker + heart cutouts on the face and cat ear silhouette on the top edge, and a slotted ledge for a phone (or CaliCat!) to sit on. It disassembles to pack flat in two pieces so that I can throw it into my bag and take it when I work (or watch YouTube) at cafes or on the train. In PrusaSlicer: Generic PLA, 0.2mm SPEED profile, Original Prusa MK4S 0.4mm nozzle, 15% infill, support enforcers only. CaliCat had a print time of 23m and used 7g of white PLA, while the full cat phone stand took 1h35m and 60g of emerald green filament. Colin also made a CaliCat, so our creatures took a pic together :)

Time Log: Finding and vetting the reference model took maybe 20 minutes. Modeling and adapting the stand from a plain rectangle to a cute cat in TinkerCAD took about 45 min. Slicing was quick (15 min), and the print ran 1h35m unattended. Troubleshooting and fixing print/file errors took about 2 hours, though, just by nature of the machine and process.

Reflection: I most clearly learned how to use a 3D printer, but also learned more about the process: print bed type (smooth, satin powder-coated, steel) matters for print adhesion and the CAD file should accurately represent the distance from the print bed. The latter was particularly frustrating, as initial prints were ‘off’ because the pieces were slightly hovering above the print bed in the CAD software.
